## Structural traps

### Paying to acquire customers who leave after a few boxes (high risk)

Meal kits already struggle with churn, and students are price-sensitive and have cheap alternatives such as supermarket meal deals and cooking from scratch. If each student stays only a few weeks, a £25 box cannot repay the cost of winning them.

**Seen before at:** Chef'd (US), Munchery (US)

**What to do about it:** Measure payback per student cohort before you spend on growth. Test term-length commitments or shared flat subscriptions so one signup brings in several recurring customers.

### No pricing headroom against heavily discounted incumbents (high risk)

Gousto and HelloFresh UK have offered up to 60% off a first box, and Gousto's losses reached almost £160m in 2022. A £25 budget box competes with those offers, and you cannot match their subsidies. Meanwhile Mindful Chef's gross margin slipped to 25.1%, which suggests how thin margins are for a premium player, let alone a budget one.

**Seen before at:** Munchery (US), Just Add Cooking (US)

**What to do about it:** Work out your gross margin per box after ingredients, packing and delivery, and stress-test it against a 10% rise in food costs. If it only works at a price students will not pay, do not launch.

### Running out of money while sales still grow (high risk)

Meal-kit economics need cash to cover acquisition, packing and delivery long before they turn profitable. A student-priced product earns less per box to fund that gap.

**Seen before at:** Just Add Cooking (US), Chef'd (US)

**What to do about it:** Plan funding for at least two academic years. Keep fixed costs low by using a co-packer rather than your own kitchen, and set clear break-even milestones before raising more.

### Demand that follows the term calendar (medium risk)

Students leave for Christmas, Easter and summer, and some will move out of halls after the first year. Your customer base would shrink and refresh on a fixed cycle, which makes forecasting orders and ingredient purchases harder. Allplants shows how a business built on a demand spike can struggle when demand returns to normal.

**Seen before at:** Allplants (UK)

**What to do about it:** Build pause and skip into the product, forecast by term, and consider a second customer group such as postgraduates or young professionals to smooth the troughs.

### Pivoting repeatedly while trying to change eating habits (medium risk)

Persuading students to cook weekly from a box means changing a habit, and many will default to takeaways or cheap convenience food. If early retention is poor, the temptation is to pivot again and again.

**Seen before at:** Housebites and Dinnr (UK)

**What to do about it:** Define in advance what retention figure means the idea is working. Test with a small number of halls and fix the product before widening the offer.

## Ways to change the odds

### Sell through the university rather than to individual students

Offer the boxes as a catered option or partnership with accommodation providers, so acquisition happens in bulk and not through paid ads.

### Shared-flat boxes

Sell a larger box to a flat of four to six students who split the cost. One signup brings in several weekly meals and reduces churn.

### Cheaper, simpler product

Drop the box and the app and sell pre-portioned ingredient packs for a few staple dishes, pickup-only from campus locations. This cuts delivery and packaging costs.

### Term-based prepaid plans

Sell a ten-week plan up front instead of a rolling weekly subscription. You collect cash early and avoid students leaving after a few boxes.
